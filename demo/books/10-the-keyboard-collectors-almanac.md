# The Keyboard Collector's Almanac

*by Wynn Halloran, 2020*

## Chapter 1: Why Anyone Collects Keyboards

A keyboard is the one part of a computer that your hands touch for hours every day, and that fact explains the whole hobby. A collector is simply a person who has noticed how a keyboard feels and decided that the difference matters. Some come for the sound, a crisp patter like rain on a tin roof. Some come for the typing feel. Some come for the beauty of an old board with a heavy steel plate and keycaps worn smooth by a stranger's fingers. Whatever the reason, the habit begins with a single question: why does this board feel different from that one?

The answer lies in the *switch*, the small mechanism under each key. A *mechanical* keyboard has one separate switch per key, each with a spring, a stem that slides up and down, and a pair of metal contacts that touch when the key is pressed far enough. A cheaper *membrane* keyboard uses a sheet of rubber domes over printed circuits, and the same dome serves as the spring. Membrane boards are quiet and inexpensive. They also tend to feel soft and vague, and that vagueness is what drives people toward the mechanical kind.

The point at which the board registers a press is the *actuation point*, and it usually lies partway down the key's travel, well above the bottom. The force needed to reach it is measured in grams or centinewtons, and typical figures run from about forty-five to sixty-five grams. A light switch suits a fast typist with small hands. A heavier one guards against accidental presses by a resting finger.

This almanac is arranged by season of a collector's life: first the switches, then the keycaps, then the keys that confuse everyone. Read it in order or dip in wherever you are stuck.

## Chapter 2: The Three Families of Switch

Nearly every mechanical switch belongs to one of three families, and learning to tell them apart is the first skill a collector needs.

The *linear* switch is the simplest. The key travels smoothly from top to bottom with an even resistance, no bump, no click. Linear switches are favored by gamers, who like to press quickly and repeatedly, and by anyone who wants a quiet, smooth action. Because nothing signals the actuation point to your finger, linear switches are easy to bottom out, driving the key hard against the base with every stroke. That makes noise and tires the hand.

The *tactile* switch adds a small bump in the middle of the travel, right where the key actuates. You feel the bump, and you know the press has registered, so you can let up without pushing to the bottom. Many typists find this the best compromise between feeling and fatigue, and a tactile switch with a gentle bump can make a long writing session considerably easier on the fingers.

The *clicky* switch adds a sound to the bump, produced by a small moving part inside the housing that snaps as the key passes the actuation point. The click is deliberate and loud. People who love it call it satisfying, and people who share an office with them call it something else. If you work near others, test a clicky board with a patient friend before you buy it.

Within each family there are many variations: stiffer or lighter springs, longer or shorter travel, and housings made of different plastics that change the pitch of the sound. Some collectors *lube* their switches, brushing a thin film of grease onto the stem and spring to remove scratchiness and dampen rattle. It is slow work, done one switch at a time, and the results can be remarkable.

Many boards now allow *hot-swapping*: the switches simply pull out and push in, with no soldering. A hot-swap board lets you try all three families on the same frame, and I recommend one to every beginner for exactly that reason.

## Chapter 3: Caps Lock, Num Lock and the Keys No One Understands

Some keys on the board carry history, and none more than **Caps Lock**. On a mechanical typewriter, raising the carriage to print capital letters took effort, so a lever called the shift lock held the mechanism raised and kept the capitals coming. The computer key inherits the idea. Press it once and every letter you type is capitalized until you press it again. A small indicator lamp, usually on the key or the board's corner, shows whether the setting is on.

The key is also, for many typists, a nuisance, because it sits where a little finger strays easily, and a sentence typed with it accidentally on emerges in the wrong case. Collectors have thus developed a small tradition of remapping it. The usual choices are to turn it into a second control key, which suits people who use keyboard shortcuts all day, or into an escape key. Most boards with programmable firmware let you do this without any software on the computer at all.

**Num Lock** is the key's cousin and has a more technical purpose. On a full-size board, the cluster of keys at the right, the *numeric keypad*, serves two jobs. With Num Lock on, the keys type digits and arithmetic symbols, convenient for anyone entering long columns of figures. With it off, the same keys act as cursor and navigation keys: arrows, Home, End, Page Up and Page Down. The design dates from the days of boards that lacked a separate navigation cluster, and it survives on the compact ones that still need it.

**Scroll Lock** is the forgotten third. It was meant to make the arrow keys scroll the display instead of moving the cursor, and nowadays it does something in only a few programs. Collectors fond of old boards like to keep it for the indicator lamp.

Ask a collector about *rollover*, and you will hear a longer answer. Rollover describes how many keys the board can recognize when they are held down together. A board with *two-key rollover* is satisfied when you press two keys at once; a fast typist, whose fingers overlap from one letter to the next, will see letters dropped when they exceed the limit. A board with *six-key rollover* is the usual standard for the common connection protocol on cheap boards, adequate for nearly everyone. *N-key rollover* means every key can be pressed at once and all of them recognized. It requires both a diode beside each switch and a more capable connection method. Gamers prize it. Typists seldom need it.

## Chapter 4: Keycaps, Cases and Care

The keycap is the part you actually touch, and it comes in two common plastics. *ABS* is smooth and takes bright colors well, but it develops a shine from the oils of the fingers over months of use. *PBT* is denser and more textured, resists the shine, and gives a deeper sound. Collectors usually prefer PBT and replace the caps of a board they like.

The legends, the letters printed on the caps, are applied by several methods. Printing the letter on the surface is cheap, and it wears off first. *Dye-sublimation* soaks the ink into the plastic and lasts longer. *Double-shot* molding makes the legend from a second piece of plastic that runs through the cap, so it cannot wear away at all. Each costs more than the last.

Keycap *profile* describes the shape and height of the rows. Some profiles are sculpted, with each row angled differently to meet the fingers, while others are flat across every row. Neither is correct; the right choice is whatever lets your hands sit comfortably. A set of caps from one profile will not fit a board that expects another, so check before buying.

For care, pull the caps with a simple wire puller, brush out the dust with a soft brush, and wash the caps in warm water with mild soap. Dry them completely before you put them back. Never pour liquid onto the board. If you spill a drink on one, unplug it at once, turn it upside down to drain, and let it dry for several days before trying it again.

Finally, the case. A heavy metal case adds weight and a solid sound, and a plastic one is lighter and livelier. Foam inside the case, between the plate and the base, mutes the hollow ring that many boards have. Try these changes one at a time, so that you can learn what each does. The collection will grow by itself, and you may as well learn as you go.
